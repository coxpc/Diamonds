Hit Better. Know Why.

Batting Mechanics Guide · 14–16 · Softball

01
The Problem Why Striding Hurts Your Swing
A big stride steals your power before you even swing.

When you stride too far, your weight shifts forward too early — your body is already moving toward the pitcher before the ball even leaves her hand. Now you're committed. Off-speed pitch? Curveball? You're way out in front and all you can do is wave at it.

Think of it like trying to punch someone while already falling toward them — your fist might land, but there's nothing behind it. Real power comes from staying back and exploding forward at the right moment.
What Actually Happens

Striding is when a batter takes a large forward step toward the pitcher as part of their load. The problem isn't the step — it's what the step does to your weight distribution and timing. A big stride forces your hips to open prematurely, your weight dumps forward, and you lose the tension in your core that generates bat speed.

Why It Kills Timing

At the 14–16 level, pitchers are throwing changeups, drops, and rises. A big stride locks you into one timing window. The moment your front foot lands heavy, your swing is basically already triggered — your brain committed. Against an off-speed pitch, your stride has already eaten your reaction time.

The Fix: Soft Stride or Toe Tap
  • Keep the stride short (4–6 inches) or replace it with a simple toe tap to trigger timing.
  • Land on the ball of your front foot softly — don't "stomp" your weight forward.
  • Your weight should stay centered or slightly back until you decide to swing.
  • Hip rotation drives power — not forward momentum. Stay back, rotate hard.
❌ Big Stride

Weight dumps forward early, hips open too soon, bat drag, weak contact, helpless on off-speed.

✅ Controlled Load

Weight stays back, hips stay closed until launch, explosive rotation, adjustable to any pitch speed.

02
The Advantage Why an Open Stance Works
An open stance lets you see the ball longer and react faster.

When your front foot is pulled back slightly from the plate, both eyes face the pitcher more directly. You're not turning your head as far to track the ball — which means you pick it up sooner out of the hand. That split second is everything at this level.

It's like watching a movie. If you're sitting dead-center in the theater, you see the full screen easily. Sit too far to the side and you're craning your neck — you'll miss things. An open stance puts you in the center seat.
What "Open Stance" Means

In an open stance, the front foot is pulled back several inches away from the plate relative to the back foot. This rotates the hips slightly toward the pitcher before the pitch even arrives. It does not mean your feet are wide apart — it means your body angle is more "open" to face the field.

Vision Advantage

Human eyes work best when both are tracking together. In a square or closed stance, batters must rotate their head significantly to pick up the ball's release. An open stance aligns both eyes more naturally toward the pitcher, giving you earlier pitch recognition — you see spin, location, and speed sooner.

Hip Turn & Power

Because the hips are already partially open, the swing rotation feels more natural and complete. For hitters who struggle to "clear their hips" on an inside pitch, an open stance removes that barrier — your body isn't fighting itself to rotate through the ball.

Foot Placement — Right-Handed Batter
Open Stance vs Square Stance
  • Front foot pulls back 4–8 inches from where it would be in a square stance.
  • Both feet still point comfortably toward the plate — don't point the front foot at the pitcher.
  • Weight stays balanced — this isn't a wide stance, just an angled one.
  • Hip rotation is natural and full, making it easier to drive the inside pitch.
03
The Mistake Don't Show the Pitcher Your Back Shoulder
A closed stance boxes you in — and the pitcher knows it.

When your front foot crosses toward the plate and your body angles away, your back shoulder starts "showing" toward the pitcher. That means your hips are already half-closed before you even load. The result? You can't rotate fully — you're swinging in a box. Pitchers at this level will throw outside and watch you wave.

Imagine trying to throw a punch while you're already turned sideways, facing a wall. Your elbow catches on the wall before you can fully extend. That's what a closed stance does to your swing — the wall is your own body.
What "Showing Your Back Shoulder" Means

In a closed stance, the front foot steps toward the plate, angling the body away from the pitcher. This causes the back shoulder to rotate toward the pitcher prematurely, and the front hip to jam against your rotation path. You've essentially pre-rotated your body in the wrong direction before the pitch arrives.

Why It Limits Power

Power in softball hitting is a kinetic chain — it flows from the ground up: legs → hips → core → shoulders → arms → bat. A closed stance breaks that chain at the hips. Your front side can't "open" properly through the zone because it's already fighting against its own starting position. The result is a short, armsy swing with no hip drive behind it.

What Pitchers See & Exploit

A smart pitcher at this level will notice a closed stance. It telegraphs that you can't cover the outside corner well — your body is turned away from it. Expect a steady diet of outside pitches, which you'll either reach for weakly or miss entirely. A closed batter is also more susceptible to high-inside heat because the stance restricts your arms from extending on that plane.

❌ Closed Stance

Front foot crosses plate-side, back shoulder faces pitcher, hips locked, restricted rotation, weak on outside, exploited by smart pitchers.

✅ Open or Square Stance

Hips free to rotate fully, back shoulder stays back until swing is triggered, power path unobstructed, covers full strike zone.

Think of your swing like a coiled spring. An open/square stance lets you fully coil. A closed stance is like pinching one end of the spring before you even load it — you've already wasted half the energy before you uncoil.
04
Know Your Body Right Eye Dominant? Here's How to Stand
Your right eye is your captain — give it the best view of the pitch.

As a right-handed batter, your right eye is in the back — farther from the pitcher. If it's also your dominant eye, that's a problem: your strongest eye has the worst angle. The fix is a slight chin tuck toward your left shoulder and an open stance, which rotates your head just enough to bring that right eye forward and lock it onto the pitcher's release point.

Think of your dominant eye like a sniper scope — it needs to be aimed directly at the target. If the scope is mounted sideways, even the best shooter misses. Adjusting your head angle is like re-mounting the scope so it points straight at the muzzle.
What Eye Dominance Actually Means

Your dominant eye is the one your brain relies on more heavily when depth perception and fine tracking matter. It's not about which eye sees "better" — it's about which eye your brain trusts to judge distance and movement. When tracking a pitched ball, your dominant eye is doing the heavy lifting on reading spin and judging when the ball reaches the zone.

The Cross-Dominance Problem

A right-handed batter naturally sets up with their left side facing the pitcher. That puts the left eye closer to the pitcher and the right eye tucked behind. For a right-eye dominant batter, this means your captain eye is riding the bench. Your weaker left eye is getting first look at every pitch, and your brain is playing catch-up to confirm what it sees. This is called cross-dominance — batting right but seeing better with the right eye — and it's more common than most players realize.

The Adjustment: Head Tilt + Open Stance

Two small tweaks work together to solve this. First, tuck your chin toward your left (front) shoulder as you set up in the box. This rotates your head slightly so both eyes angle more directly toward the pitcher — and your right eye gets a cleaner sight line to the release point. Second, pair this with an open stance (front foot pulled back, as covered in Card 02). The open stance rotates your whole body slightly toward the pitcher, which multiplies the benefit of the head tilt. Together, they bring the dominant right eye into its optimal tracking position.

How to Check Your Dominant Eye
  • Point your finger at a distant object with both eyes open.
  • Close your left eye — if your finger stays on the object, you're right eye dominant.
  • If your finger jumps off the object when you close your left eye, you're left eye dominant.
  • Do this test before your next practice so you know exactly what you're working with.
The Full Checklist for Right-Eye Dominant Batters
  • Open stance — pull the front foot back to rotate your body slightly toward the pitcher.
  • Chin toward left shoulder — tuck it as you set up, not as a reaction to the pitch.
  • Both eyes forward — you should feel like you're looking almost face-on at the pitcher, not in profile.
  • Don't overdo it — a 10–15° head rotation is enough. You're not trying to face the pitcher fully; you're just giving the right eye its lane.
Your two eyes work like two cameras feeding one screen. When your dominant eye has a clear angle, the image is crisp and the depth is accurate. When it's blocked or angled away, your brain is stitching together a blurry, delayed picture — and trying to hit a 60 mph drop ball with bad footage is a losing game.
